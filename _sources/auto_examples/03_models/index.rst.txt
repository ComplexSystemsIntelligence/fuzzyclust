Clustering for model identification
-----------------------------------

Fuzzy clusters in the product space of inputs and outputs are local models:
Takagi–Sugeno fuzzy models of static functions and of SISO / MIMO dynamic
systems (modified Gath–Geva clustering), switching regression with fuzzy
c-regression models, fuzzy classifiers from supervised clustering, supervised
fuzzy partitions for decision trees, and hinging-hyperplane regression trees.

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Rule- and tree-induction methods of the ID3 family work on discrete attributes, so continuous features are partitioned before the tree is grown. The usual a priori partitions (equal-width bins, equal-frequency bins, or a Ruspini partition of triangular sets) ignore the class labels, so their cut points fall anywhere. Abonyi (2010) proposed deriving the partition from the supervised fuzzy clustering of the previous example. Every rule&#x27;s Gaussian antecedent set, projected onto a feature, is a fuzzy set of that feature; the Gaussians are turned into trapezoids with the corner points v-3\sigma,\ v-\sigma/2,\ v+\sigma/2,\ v+3\sigma; similar sets (Jaccard similarity above a threshold \theta) are merged; and a feature left with a single set is a don&#x27;t care variable. The cut points therefore lie where the classes change. This example shows the partitions, feeds their crisp codes and their memberships to a scikit-learn decision tree, and compares the result with unsupervised discretizations.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A hinging hyperplane (Breiman) is the continuous piecewise-linear function h(\mathbf{x})=\max(\mathbf{x}_e^T\boldsymbol\theta_1,\ \mathbf{x}_e^T\boldsymbol\theta_2) or the corresponding \min: two planes glued together along the hinge \{\mathbf{x}:\ \mathbf{x}_e^T(\boldsymbol\theta_1-\boldsymbol\theta_2)=0\}. Kenesei and Abonyi observed that fuzzy c-regression with c=2 finds two local linear models and their fuzzy operating regions at once, that is, one hinge, provided the two planes cross inside the data (a constraint added to the weighted least-squares step). The sign of the hinge function then splits the data into the two operating regions, each of which is again modeled by a hinge, and so on. The result is a binary regression tree with oblique splits whose leaves are hinge functions. No splitting variable has to be chosen, the tree is piecewise linear, and every node reports how well the crisp split agrees with the fuzzy partition. This example fits a surface made of two crossing hinges and studies the depth, the leaf size, and the growth strategy.">

Fuzzy self-organizing map: ordered prototypes by regularized FCM#

Fuzzy c-means places its prototypes wherever the data are dense, in no particular order. The fuzzy self-organizing map of Abonyi, Migály, and Szeifert arranges the prototypes on the nodes of a low-dimensional grid and adds a smoothness penalty to the FCM functional,

\[J = \sum_{i=1}^{c}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu_{ik}^m\|\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{v}_i\|^2 + \vartheta\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{V}^T\mathbf{L}\mathbf{V}),\]

where \(\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{G}^T\mathbf{G}\) is built from second-order finite differences of the prototypes along the grid axes. The membership update is unchanged; the prototype update becomes a linear system that couples neighboring nodes. The result is a topology-preserving map like Kohonen’s SOM, with the advantages of a fuzzy partition and of an objective function that is minimized exactly. This example reproduces the two geometric case studies of the paper, shows the effect of \(\vartheta\) and of the order of the difference operator, and draws the U-matrix and component planes of a map of the wine data.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.distance import pdist
from scipy.stats import spearmanr
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

from fuzzyclust.datasets import load_wine
from fuzzyclust.viz import FuzzySOM, make_hemisphere, make_spiral_3d, plot_map, plot_som_lattice

A chain of prototypes along a curve#

The data are 300 noisy points on one turn of a helix in 3-D. Seven prototypes on a one-dimensional grid are initialized on the first principal axis. Plain FCM (\(\vartheta=0\)) finds seven groups along the curve but connects them in a tangled order; with the penalty, the chain follows the curve.

X, t = make_spiral_3d(n_samples=300, turns=1.0, noise=0.2, random_state=0)

# [snippet:chain]
fcm_chain = FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(7,), regularization=0.0, random_state=0).fit(X)
som_chain = FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(7,), regularization=2.0, random_state=0).fit(X)
# [/snippet:chain]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4.8), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, model, name in zip(axes, (fcm_chain, som_chain), ("FCM (regularization = 0)", "fuzzy SOM (regularization = 2)"),
                           strict=True):
    plot_som_lattice(model, X, dims=(0, 1), ax=ax)
    ax.set_title(f"{name}: J = {model.objective_:.1f}")
plt.tight_layout()

A 6 x 6 map folded onto a hemisphere#

The data are 900 points on the upper half of the unit sphere (noise 0.1). The lattice of prototypes, drawn in the \((x_1, x_2)\) plane, shows whether the map is ordered: with \(\vartheta=0\) the grid edges cross; with \(\vartheta=0.5\) the lattice is a regular net (paper, Figs. 4 and 6). The Spearman correlation between grid distances and prototype distances summarizes the ordering in one number.

Xh, uv = make_hemisphere(n_samples=900, noise=0.1, random_state=0)

# [snippet:hemisphere]
maps = {th: FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(6, 6), regularization=th, random_state=0).fit(Xh) for th in (0.0, 0.5, 5.0)}
# [/snippet:hemisphere]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(16, 5), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, (th, model) in zip(axes, maps.items(), strict=True):
    rho = spearmanr(pdist(model.grid_positions_), pdist(model.cluster_centers_)).correlation
    plot_som_lattice(model, Xh, dims=(0, 1), ax=ax)
    ax.set_title(rf"$\vartheta$ = {th}:  J = {model.objective_:.2f},  ordering $\rho$ = {rho:.2f}")
plt.tight_layout()

The regularization costs little in the data term but produces an ordered map; a very large \(\vartheta\) flattens the map into a nearly planar grid that no longer follows the curvature of the hemisphere (the prototypes’ third coordinate shrinks). The component planes make this visible: each plane shows one coordinate of the prototypes over the grid.

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 3.8))
for j, ax in enumerate(axes):
    plot_map(maps[0.5], kind="component", feature=j, ax=ax)
    ax.set_title(rf"$x_{j + 1}$ over the grid ($\vartheta$ = 0.5)")
plt.tight_layout()

Second-order versus first-order differences#

The “smoothly distributed FCM” of Pascual-Marqui et al. penalizes the first-order differences (the lengths of the grid edges). This attracts all prototypes toward the center of the data. With the second-order operator, only the curvature of the map is penalized, and a regular planar grid costs nothing. The extent of the map shrinks with \(\vartheta\) in the first case and stays constant in the second. [snippet:order]

thetas = (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0)
extent = {order: [np.ptp(FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(6, 6), regularization=th, difference_order=order,
                                  random_state=0).fit(Xh).cluster_centers_, axis=0).max()
                  for th in thetas] for order in (1, 2)}
# [/snippet:order]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.5, 4))
for order, style in ((2, "o-"), (1, "s--")):
    ax.plot(thetas, extent[order], style, lw=1.8, label=f"difference order {order}")
ax.set_xlabel(r"regularization weight $\vartheta$")
ax.set_ylabel("largest extent of the prototypes")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
ax.grid(True, color="0.9")
plt.tight_layout()

U-matrix and component planes of real data#

A 6 x 6 map is fitted to the standardized wine data (13 attributes). In high dimensions with many prototypes, the memberships for \(m=2\) become almost uniform, so a smaller exponent (\(m=1.3\)) is used to give the map contrast. The U-matrix shows the mean distance of every prototype to its grid neighbors: dark ridges separate groups of similar prototypes, that is, clusters. The fuzzy hit histogram shows where the data fall, and the component planes of two attributes reveal which variables separate the groups.

wine = load_wine()
Xw = StandardScaler().fit_transform(wine.data)
# [snippet:wine_som]
som_w = FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(6, 6), m=1.3, regularization=0.5, random_state=0).fit(Xw)
# [/snippet:wine_som]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(18, 4))
plot_map(som_w, kind="umatrix", ax=axes[0])
plot_map(som_w, kind="hits", ax=axes[1])
plot_map(som_w, kind="component", feature=wine.feature_names.index("flavanoids"), ax=axes[2])
axes[2].set_title("flavanoids")
plot_map(som_w, kind="component", feature=wine.feature_names.index("color_intensity"), ax=axes[3])
axes[3].set_title("color intensity")
plt.tight_layout()

The hit histograms of the three cultivars show that each occupies its own region of the map, separated by the U-matrix ridges, and the planes show the chemistry behind the split: high flavanoid content on one side, high color intensity on another.

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 3.8))
for cls, ax in enumerate(axes):
    plot_map(som_w, kind="hits", X=Xw[wine.target == cls], ax=ax)
    h = som_w.hits(Xw[wine.target == cls])
    i, j = np.unravel_index(h.argmax(), h.shape)
    ax.set_title(f"hits of cultivar {cls + 1} (peak at node ({i}, {j}))")
plt.tight_layout()

Parameter study: grid size and fuzziness exponent on the hemisphere#

Besides \(\vartheta\), a map has two structural parameters. The grid size fixes the number of prototypes. More nodes lower the data term of \(J\) and, with \(\vartheta=0.5\), keep the map ordered (Spearman \(\rho\) between grid and prototype distances close to 1), whereas the unregularized lattices of the same size are less ordered (\(\rho\) between 0.79 and 0.95). The exponent \(m\) acts in the familiar way, with one consequence that matters for maps: as \(m\) grows, the memberships of the 36 prototypes approach \(1/c\), all prototypes are pulled toward the center of the data, and the map shrinks (the range of the third coordinate collapses). This is why the wine map above was fitted with \(m=1.3\).

# [snippet:grid_m_scan]
print("grid    theta   objective J   data term   ordering rho   range of x3")
for size in (3, 4, 6, 8):
    for th in (0.0, 0.5):
        som = FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(size, size), regularization=th, random_state=0).fit(Xh)
        d2 = ((Xh[:, None, :] - som.cluster_centers_[None]) ** 2).sum(axis=-1)
        data_term = (som.membership_ ** som.m * d2).sum()
        rho = spearmanr(pdist(som.grid_positions_), pdist(som.cluster_centers_)).correlation
        print(f"{size}x{size}   {th:5.1f}   {som.objective_:11.2f}   {data_term:9.2f}   {rho:12.2f}"
              f"   {np.ptp(som.cluster_centers_[:, 2]):11.2f}")

print("\nexponent m   ordering rho   partition coefficient   range of x3   (6 x 6 grid, theta = 0.5)")
for m in (1.3, 1.6, 2.0, 2.5):
    som = FuzzySOM(grid_shape=(6, 6), m=m, regularization=0.5, random_state=0).fit(Xh)
    rho = spearmanr(pdist(som.grid_positions_), pdist(som.cluster_centers_)).correlation
    pc = np.mean(som.membership_ ** 2) * som.cluster_centers_.shape[0]
    print(f"{m:10.1f}   {rho:12.2f}   {pc:21.3f}   {np.ptp(som.cluster_centers_[:, 2]):11.2f}")

Take-away#

The fuzzy SOM is FCM with one extra term, a smoothness penalty that couples the prototypes along a grid and turns an unordered set of cluster centers into a topology-preserving map; the functional is still minimized exactly by alternating optimization. A moderate \(\vartheta\) buys the ordering for a small increase in the data term (under ten percent here), whereas a large one flattens the map. The second-order operator leaves a regular planar grid unpenalized, while the first-order penalty of smoothly distributed FCM contracts it. The grid size sets the resolution. With many prototypes in many dimensions the exponent must be kept small; otherwise the memberships flatten and the map collapses to the center of the data. The U-matrix, hit histograms, and component planes are then read as for a Kohonen map, with a fuzzy partition attached.

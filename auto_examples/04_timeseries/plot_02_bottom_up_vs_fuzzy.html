Bottom-up segmentation with PCA models versus fuzzy segmentation#

The classical way to segment a time series is bottom-up: start from a fine partition into short pieces, compute the cost of merging every pair of adjacent segments, merge the cheapest pair, and repeat until the cheapest merge would cost more than a tolerance or until the desired number of segments is left. For multivariate data, the cost of a segment is the error of one PCA model fitted to it: the mean reconstruction error \(Q\) (distance from the hyperplane) or the mean Hotelling \(T^2\) (spread inside the hyperplane). This is the baseline against which the segmentation paper compares, and its two costs see different things: \(Q\) reacts to changes in the correlation structure, \(T^2\) to shifts of the operating point. The fuzzy segmentation combines both in one model.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

from fuzzyclust.plotting import PALETTE
from fuzzyclust.timeseries import FuzzySegmentation, bottom_up_segmentation, make_piecewise_timeseries

Data: a correlation change, a mean shift, and a change of both#

X, true_segments = make_piecewise_timeseries(
    n_samples=2000,
    change_points=(500, 1000, 1500),
    change_kinds=("correlation", "mean", "both"),
    noise=0.15,
    random_state=0,
)
t = np.arange(X.shape[0])
true_borders = (500, 1000, 1500)

Bottom-up segmentation with the two costs#

Both runs are stopped at four segments so that the results are comparable with the fuzzy segmentation. The merge costs recorded along the way jump at the moment the algorithm starts to merge pieces of different regimes.

# [snippet:fit]
bu_q = bottom_up_segmentation(X, n_components=2, n_segments=4, cost="q", init_length=20)
bu_t2 = bottom_up_segmentation(X, n_components=2, n_segments=4, cost="t2", init_length=20)
fuzzy = FuzzySegmentation(n_segments=4, n_components=2).fit(X)
print("bottom-up, Q   :", bu_q.boundaries)
print("bottom-up, T^2 :", bu_t2.boundaries)
print("fuzzy          :", fuzzy.boundaries_)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(4, 1, figsize=(9, 6.5), sharex=True)
axes[0].plot(t, X[:, 0], color="0.3", lw=0.6)

axes[0].set_ylabel("$x_1$, $x_4$")
axes[0].set_title("Two of the six variables and the segment borders found by three methods")
for ax, borders, name, col in zip(
    axes[1:],
    (bu_q.boundaries, bu_t2.boundaries, fuzzy.boundaries_),

):
    for cp in true_borders:

    for b in borders:

axes[-1].set_xlabel("time")

The \(Q\) cost finds the correlation changes (at 500 and 1500) and misses the pure mean shift at 1000. The data of the second and third regimes lie on the same hyperplane, only displaced along it, so one PCA model reconstructs both without error. The \(T^2\) cost finds the mean shifts (1000, 1500) and misses the pure correlation change at 500, because the spread inside the hyperplane does not change there. The fuzzy segmentation detects all three.

The merge costs show where to stop#

Plotted against the number of segments left, the cost of each merge rises slowly while pieces of the same regime are joined and jumps when the algorithm has to join different regimes. The knee marks the natural number of segments. Here it is read off the \(Q\) curve at three (the mean shift is invisible to \(Q\)) and off the \(T^2\) curve at three as well (the correlation change is invisible to \(T^2\)).

full_q = bottom_up_segmentation(X, n_components=2, n_segments=1, cost="q", init_length=20)
full_t2 = bottom_up_segmentation(X, n_components=2, n_segments=1, cost="t2", init_length=20)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.4))
for ax, res, name, col in zip(
    axes, (full_q, full_t2), ("$Q$ cost", "$T^2$ cost"), (PALETTE[0], PALETTE[2]), strict=True
):
    n_left = np.arange(len(res.merge_costs), 0, -1)  # segments left after each merge
    ax.semilogy(n_left, res.merge_costs, "o-", color=col, ms=3, lw=1.2)
    ax.set_xlim(12.5, 0.5)
    ax.set_xlabel("segments left after the merge")
    ax.set_ylabel("cost of the merged segment")
    ax.set_title(f"Merge costs of the bottom-up algorithm, {name}")
    ax.grid(True, color="0.9", which="both")
plt.tight_layout()

Parameter study: number of components, cost, and initial piece length#

The \(Q\) cost depends strongly on \(q\). With \(q=2\), the dimension of the hidden process, a mean shift along the hyperplane is invisible. With \(q=1\) the displacement between two regimes leaves the one-dimensional model and is detected as well, by accident rather than by design. With \(q=5\) the residual is almost pure noise and the borders become unreliable. The \(T^2\) cost is insensitive to \(q\) but never sees the correlation change. A long initial piece (40 samples) coarsens the borders to multiples of the piece length and cuts the transition at 1500 into two pieces.

# [snippet:scan]
rows = []
for cost in ("q", "t2"):
    for q in (1, 2, 3, 5):
        for init_length in (10, 40):
            res = bottom_up_segmentation(X, n_components=q, n_segments=4, cost=cost, init_length=init_length)
            found = sorted(int(b) for b in res.boundaries)
            err = [min(abs(b - cp) for b in found) for cp in true_borders]
            rows.append((cost, q, init_length, found, err))
# [/snippet:scan]
print(f"{'cost':>4} {'q':>2} {'init':>5}  borders found           error at (500, 1000, 1500)")
for cost, q, init_length, found, err in rows:
    print(f"{cost:>4} {q:>2} {init_length:>5}  {str(found):<24} {err}")

Finally, the fuzzy segmentation is run with the same number of segments. Unlike the greedy method, it is not bound by an early wrong merge:

for q in (1, 2, 3, 5):
    print(f"fuzzy, q = {q}: borders {FuzzySegmentation(n_segments=4, n_components=q).fit(X).boundaries_.tolist()}")

Take-away. The bottom-up algorithm is fast and simple, but each of its costs is blind to one kind of change. The reconstruction error does not see displacements along the hyperplane, the Hotelling statistic does not see rotations of the hyperplane, and a greedy merge made early is never undone. The fuzzy segmentation uses one probabilistic PCA model per segment that covers both subspaces and re-optimizes all segments after every merge, which makes it insensitive to the number of principal components (from \(q=2\) on). Use the bottom-up method for a quick first look and to initialize or validate the fuzzy segmentation.
